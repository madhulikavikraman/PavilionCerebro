# Pavilion Cerebro: findings for the team

**Date:** 2026-09-26 (Saturday evening, PT). **Scope:** the wow-feature identification and the six module asks (numeric AI, facade + interior walls, common-area energy, LA rain, fire de-escalation, pipe clogging), plus the local website.

**How this was made.** Each section was written from the research notes (`docs/research/11_*` to `16_*`), the evaluation files in `eval/`, and the site pages, then an adversarial fact-checker re-opened every cited file and corrected numbers, paths and sources. Every number carries its data label (REAL / SIMULATED / SYNTHETIC / INJECTED / SEMI-SYNTHETIC) and the artifact it comes from. No result here comes from a customer building, and no customer interviews are logged yet.

## TL;DR

- **Feature plan (§1):** We merged 48 raw ideas into 30 features. LLM scorers rated each one; these are opinions, not judge data. The best 3-minute story uses four features: F13 NYC Report Card, F01 Fix-First, F02 Pour Water Here and F04+F06 sign-off plus log. None of the four is fully built. F13 and F02 are not built. F01 and F04+F06 are partly built. Together they need 24.5 h, and less than a day remains before the plan's Sunday 14:00 PT code freeze. We have logged zero customer interviews.
- **Numeric AI (§2):** Chronos-2 (Hugging Face, Apache-2.0) forecasts next-day electricity with 6.19% error (WAPE). The "same hour last week" baseline scores 10.12%. Test: 20 office meters, 25,916 scored hours, **REAL** (`eval/numeric/backtest_bdg2.json`). Our laptop fallback (HGB) scores 7.00%. The "80%" band held the actual value only 0.730 of the time, so it is too narrow.
- **Numeric anomalies (§2):** A model trained on human labels reaches PR-AUC 0.630. The simple robust z-score reaches 0.178. IsolationForest scored 0.155 and did not beat the simple rule. **REAL (human labels)**, `eval/numeric/anomaly_lead.json`.
- **Facade (§3):** Our crack screen scores AUROC 0.918 on 14 held-out wall photos (3,529 tiles). The old heuristic scores 0.642. On real drone facade photos it never trained on, it scores 0.906 and the old heuristic 0.445. **REAL**, `eval/facade/tilecls_v1.json`. It is a "look here first" aid for the inspector, not a FISP finding. Nobody has measured the Claude grader's accuracy.
- **Interior walls (§3):** We estimate concrete strength from rebound hammer plus ultrasonic readings. Average error is 9.65 MPa; guessing the training mean gives 14.37 MPa. Test: 14 held-out real-structure studies. With 3 labelled samples from the same study, error drops from 9.79 to 5.47 MPa. **REAL**, `eval/interior/ndt_strength_v1.json`. A simple power law beat the boosted-tree model. There is no ML moisture model.
- **Energy (§4):** In 5 real rooms, 36.1% of recorded lighting energy (135.3 of 375.2 kWh) was used while the room was empty. **REAL**, `eval/energy/replay_robod.json`. In our simulated tower, motion sensors alone save 34.0% and sensors plus ML save 30.7%, so the ML costs 19,139 kWh a year. **SEMI-SYNTHETIC + INJECTED**, `eval/energy/tower_year.json`. We never let the ML switch lights off: ML-only switching left 6.14% of occupied time under-lit. **REAL + INJECTED**.
- **Rain (§5):** At all 7 LA basin and San Fernando Valley stations, the wettest wall faces E or SE. **REAL**, `eval/rain/summary.json`. At LAX, W and SW winds blow in 65.1% of non-calm hours but bring only 19.7% of the rain. **REAL**, `eval/rain/station_roses.json`. So an everyday wind rose points at the wrong wall. 88.7-89.5% of the driving-rain index falls from November to March, so inspect before November.
- **Rain model (§5):** It predicts rain amount per wall better than the best simple rule (average error 3.34 vs 4.20 L/m²). It is no better at naming the wettest wall: 0.428 vs 0.428 on held-out years. On real GFS forecasts it loses, 0.35 vs 0.40. **REAL held-out**, `eval/rain/metrics.json`.
- **Fire (§6):** The plan card and three drawings work. 224 of 224 cards pass the 12 rule checks, and 992 of 992 escalated cards pass. **SYNTHETIC**, and this checks rule conformance only (`eval/fire/planner_checks.json`). The AI fire-vs-nuisance verifier failed in a room it had never seen. It called 11 of 11 nuisances fire-like, the same as the simple trigger. **REAL**, `eval/fire/sensor_eval.json`. Lead with the card and the drawings.
- **Clog Watch (§7):** A short nightly 4.0 L/s test flow flags 98.1% of severe-clog nights; the BMS low-pressure alarm flags 1.8%. Moderate clogs: 49.3%. Mild clogs: only 6.2%. False alarms are 3.9% of clean nights, more than the BMS alarm's 1.8%. Labels: **SIMULATED riser, INJECTED clogs, REAL demand** (`eval/clog/riser_metrics.json`). ML did not beat the plain rule at detection. It only grades severity better: macro-F1 0.584 vs 0.492.
- **Clog, real sewer (§7):** We have one real blockage event (Bellinge). The paired rule raised 0 other alarm episodes in 3.38 held-out months. It was slower, though: 70 min after onset, vs 35 min for a fixed-level alarm, which raised 3 other episodes. **REAL (one event)**, `eval/clog/bellinge_case.json`. This is a case study.

## Do NOT say

- **"Tested in real buildings" or "accurate on real buildings."** No customer building has been tested (`home.py:203`). The fire tower is SYNTHETIC, the energy tower SEMI-SYNTHETIC and the clog riser SIMULATED. Our REAL results come from public datasets: lab rooms, airports, office meters and wall photos.
- **"Our AI detects fires," "cuts false alarms" or "fire predicted."** In the room it never saw, it called 11 of 11 nuisances fire-like, no better than the particle trigger. Stage-2 AUROC was only 0.669. The ML alone raised 139.33 false alarms per 24 sensor-hours (`eval/fire/sensor_eval.json`).
- **"The AI switches the lights" or "the AI saves 30%."** The code motion sensors do the saving: 34.0% alone vs 30.7% with ML. The ML costs 19,139 kWh a year. ML-only switching left 6.14% of occupied time under-lit, so in our design the ML can only keep lights on longer (`eval/energy/tower_year.json`, `eval/energy/replay_robod.json`).
- **"It never breaks a life-safety floor."** The recommended policy leaves stairs below 10 fc for 400 min a year (SEMI-SYNTHETIC + INJECTED, `eval/energy/tower_year.json`). The exit-route result of 0 is 0 by construction, so it proves nothing.
- **"The AI predicts which wall a storm will hit hardest" or "litres on your wall."** It ties the baseline on reanalysis (0.428 vs 0.428). On real GFS forecasts it loses (0.35 vs 0.40, `eval/rain/metrics.json`). The values are relative exposure measured at airports, not water on a real wall. We have not checked them against leak records.
- **"AI detects clogs" or "prevents backups."** Every riser and drain number is SIMULATED with INJECTED clogs. The detection came from a plain z-score rule, not ML. Mild clogs were flagged on only 6.2% of nights. False alarms (3.9%) were higher than the BMS alarm's (1.8%). Bellinge is one sewer event, and the paired rule there was slower than a fixed alarm.
- **"Our forecast range is 80% reliable," "Chronos-2 beats Chronos-Bolt" or "better on every meter."** Band coverage was 0.730. The paired Chronos-2 vs Bolt interval includes zero. Chronos-2 won on 19 of 20 meters, not all 20 (`eval/numeric/backtest_bdg2.json`).
- **"Our facade AI grades FISP conditions" or "we beat Hugging Face crack models."** The grader's accuracy is unmeasured because no labelled Safe/SWARMP/Unsafe photo set exists. We scored no Hugging Face crack model. The screen never saw wood, so it does not cover SB 721/326 balconies.
- **"We manage batteries and chillers," "four AI agents" or "a swarm of agents."** None of that is built. Battery and chiller slots (F25) are a pitch-only roadmap line, and the plan flags this wording as an overclaim.
- **"Customers told us..." or "validated with building owners."** Zero interviews are logged in the plan and in `docs/progress.md`. Give the real count, even if it is zero. The pilot protocol is proposed, not run.
- **"Every decision is logged and tamper-proof" or a live "pour water here" demo.** Energy approvals last only for the browser session. The hash-chained decision log is not built. F02 was refuted as written, and the engine still walks 2 hops from the inferred root.
- **"Runs fully offline" once someone clicks the facade grader.** The grader sends image crops to the Claude API. Also, never show LEAD series in public material: that dataset states no licence.

**What we found (research):**
- Method: 48 raw ideas from 6 lenses, merged into 30 canonical features. The whole feature workflow used 29 agents [2]. Each feature was scored 1-5 on five criteria: lay judge, VC, rubric, feasibility and honesty [1]. These are LLM scorer opinions. We recomputed all 30 composites from [1]. Each equals 0.25·lay + 0.25·VC + 0.2·rubric + 0.2·feas + 0.1·honest. The 7 features with honesty ≤ 2 get 0.6 times that score. So an exciting but misleading idea cannot win: F10 scores 4/4/4 on lay/VC/rubric and still ends at 2.16 [1].
- Verifiers attacked the top 12. They refuted 2: F02 as written, and F24 [1][2].
- Lay judges scored highest on physical moments you get at a glance: water reaching an electrical room (F02), a signed approval (F04), a phone photo turning into a pin (F14) and a blind test (F11). Each got lay 5 [1].
- VCs scored highest on features that carry go-to-market or a moat: F01 and F13 (VC 5 each) [1]. Of the four build-now features, only F13 uses real building data [1]. (Since then, the Numeric AI page has also used REAL building meters; see Measured results.) F13's lead list is buildings whose latest filing is Unsafe or SWARMP. Under 1 RCNY 103-04 (d)(3), an owner who fails to correct an unsafe condition owes a base civil penalty of $1000/month, plus a per-linear-foot shed charge from year 2 [3].
- F02 as written changes nothing, because the engine walks 2 hops from the inferred root [1]. The code still works that way today: `src/cascade/building/problems.py:563-578` (`downstream(b, root, max_hops=hops)`), `src/cascade/building/rules.py:112` (`WATER_NEAR_PANEL_HOPS` = 2).

## 2. Numeric AI: Hugging Face models for sensor numbers

**Question (verbatim from the team lead):**
> can you check for a ml model that we can use from hugging face or any similar website that can take input in numbers and make data processing on top of that for this problem to solve in any way?

**Short answer:** Yes. We use `amazon/chronos-2` (Apache-2.0, 119.48 M parameters). It forecasts each meter's next 24 hours with a 10/50/90 range and needs no training on our data. On 20 real office electricity meters its error (WAPE) was 6.19%. The rule of thumb "same hour last week" scored 10.12%, and a plain scikit-learn model (HGB) that runs live on a laptop CPU scored 7.00%. Its 80% range held the actual value only 73.0% of the time, so the range is too narrow. For flagging unusual hours, a model trained on human-labelled examples reached PR-AUC 0.630, against 0.178 for a simple robust z-score. The unsupervised IsolationForest did not beat that simple baseline (0.155).

**What we found (research):**
- **Chronos-2 is the best fit.** It is Apache-2.0 and not gated. It is zero-shot, returns quantiles and accepts covariates [1][2][3]. Pinned revision: `29ec3766d36d6f73f0696f85560a422f50e8498c`. Its weight file is 477.9 MB [5] (`eval/numeric/model_catalogue.json`). The paper claims "state-of-the-art" zero-shot results [3]. That is the authors' claim. We did not reproduce it.
- **Second opinion: `amazon/chronos-bolt-small`.** Apache-2.0, 47.72 M parameters, 190.9 MB, revision `772f3d25d38aec6d914c8949dab4462e2d46f5d8` [4][5]. It installs from the same `chronos-forecasting` package [2].
- **Live fallback: scikit-learn `HistGradientBoostingRegressor`** with a split-conformal band. It is BSD-3 licensed and already installed, so it adds no new dependency [23] (`eval/numeric/model_catalogue.json`). We also used scikit-learn IsolationForest for unsupervised anomaly scoring [24].
- **Licence traps. Do not use these in a commercial product:**
  - Moirai 2.0 is cc-by-nc-4.0, "research purposes only" [6].
  - TimesFM 3.0 weights are under a non-commercial, non-production licence. TimesFM weights up to 2.5 are still Apache-2.0 [7].
  - TabPFN 2.5, 2.6, 3 and 3.5 weights are non-commercial, and you accept the licence through a login. Only the v2 weights (Apache 2.0 plus an attribution clause) are usable [8].
  - TiRex uses the NXAI Community License. It is based on the Llama 3 licence and adds commercial terms for large enterprises [9][10].
- **Install blockers on Python 3.13 / Windows:**
  - uni2ts (for Moirai), momentfm, toto-ts and Lag-Llama pin old numpy, torch or gluonts versions [11][12][13][14]. numpy 1.26.4 has no Python 3.13 Windows wheel [15].
  - Granite TTM and TSPulse need scikit-learn <1.8 [26].
  - The Sundial card recommends transformers 4.40.1 [27].
- **Dependency conflict:** `chronos-forecasting` pulls in transformers 5.17, which needs huggingface-hub <2.0 [16]. Our runtime env has 2.0.0. So Chronos runs offline in a separate env (`cerebro_ml`), and the site reads its saved forecasts (`docs/decisions.md`).
- **Why we excluded some GIFT-Eval sites:**
  - `Salesforce/GiftEvalPretrain` contains the BDG2 sites Bear, Fox, Panther and Rat, plus the folders bull, cockatoo and hog [17].
  - The `amazon/chronos-2` card metadata lists GiftEvalPretrain among its datasets. The Chronos-2 paper's Table 6 lists no BDG-2 subset.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- Use Chronos-2 as the primary numeric model, run offline with its results saved. It had the lowest WAPE, MASE and pinball loss. It beat HGB by a paired margin of −0.81 points (95% interval −1.17 to −0.32).
- Chronos-2 and Chronos-Bolt-small are statistically tied. Bolt had the best-calibrated band (0.772) and the lowest CV(RMSE). It is also about 5.7× faster on CPU (35.98 s vs 205.37 s). If CPU time matters, Bolt is a fair choice.
- Chronos-2 lost on CV(RMSE), a measure that punishes big misses. Its 12.71% was worse than Bolt (12.18%) and HGB (12.34%). It also lost at the Wolf site.
- Keep HGB as the live CPU fallback. It is 3.12 points better than the baseline (paired interval −4.95 to −1.68) and adds no dependency.
- For anomalies, get labels. The supervised model's precision in the top 1% of alerts was 0.884, against 0.309 for the no-training baseline. Its recall there was only 0.428. It would need labels from our own buildings before we use it.
- Do not rely on IsolationForest. It did not beat robust z on PR-AUC or ROC-AUC, and it was only marginally better in the top 1% of alerts.

**Limits and what NOT to claim in the pitch:**
- Do not say the forecast range is "80% reliable". Every band under-covers (0.730 to 0.772).
- Do not say Chronos-2 "beats the baseline on every meter". It won on 19 of 20. Every model lost on Robin_office_Wai. That meter repeats almost exactly week to week in Nov–Dec, which suggests gap filling in the source file (`docs/research/11_numeric_models.md`).
- Do not say Chronos-2 is better than Chronos-Bolt-small. The paired interval includes zero.
- The test covers only 20 office meters from 4 sites in one winter window (Nov–Dec 2017). Other building types and seasons are unmeasured. The intervals resample meters but ignore the 4-site clustering, so they are likely too narrow for a new building.
- Only univariate electricity forecasting was measured. Humidity, water flow, weather and occupancy inputs were **not** measured, even though the catalogue lists them as possible uses.
- Do not claim zero pretraining overlap. We did not check `autogluon/chronos_datasets`, and Bolt's card does not name its training data. Do not say "no model saw these weeks". That is true for our HGB, but unknown for Chronos.
- Do not call the BDG2 flags "detections". They have no labels.
- The supervised anomaly model was trained and tested on LEAD only, and it is not saved in the repo.
- Do not show LEAD series in public material. LEAD has no stated licence. Keep `CEREBRO_LOCAL_LEAD` unset for any demo or screenshot.
- The derived BDG2 file (`eval/numeric/bdg2_selected.parquet`) carries CC BY-SA share-alike.
- The site does not run Chronos live. It reads saved forecasts, and only HGB re-runs on the page.
- `NumericAgent` is **not** wired into the building problem list (`src/cascade/building/`). We found no import of it there.
- One selection rule was changed during the build: at most 5% flat days before the test window. The first draw had included effectively dead Lamb meters (for example, Lamb_office_Gerardo read 0.0018 kWh from April to October 2017).
  - A 1% limit was tried first. It would have left 24 eligible meters, and it was rejected.
  - The builder's log says this happened before any backtest metric was computed. We cannot prove that from the artifacts. An earlier research pilot on a different draw did compute metrics.
  - Details: `eval/numeric/backtest_setup.json` `selection_diagnostics`.
- All timings come from a shared laptop with other jobs running.

## 3. Exterior facade inspection and interior wall testing

**Question (verbatim from the team lead):**
> "we need to have a website, so our crack detection from the previous build is ready, mold it to have it done for the exterior inspection of the buildings, research for the data captured for interior testing of the walls and make a ML model for that."

**Short answer:** Both pages are on the local website, under "Inspect". The exterior page runs a crack screen we trained ourselves: a ResNet-18, stored as a 22.4 MB ONNX file. It shades a facade photo and lists the squares an inspector should look at first. If the user asks, those squares then go to the crack grader and width tool from the previous build. We tested the screen on real photos it never saw during training. It scored AUROC 0.918 on close-range wall photos and 0.906 on real drone facade photos. Of the interior wall tests we reviewed, only the rebound hammer and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) have a large public dataset paired with measured concrete strength. So we built a concrete-strength estimator from those two readings. On held-out real-structure (in-situ) studies its average error is 9.65 MPa. With 3 labelled samples from the same study, the error drops to 5.47 MPa. A simple power-law formula beat the boosted-tree model.

**What we found (research):**
- NYC FISP has the inspector (QEWI) rate each significant condition Safe, SWARMP or Unsafe. The rule says: "The use of drones, high resolution photography, non-destructive testing, or other similar methods does not eliminate the requirements for close-up inspections" [1]. So our screen can only help the QEWI decide where to look and map the facade. It never files anything.
- California SB 721 [2] covers balconies, decks, stairways and walkways more than 6 ft up that rely on wood for support. Our research notes treat SB 326 [3] (condo associations) as covering the same kind of wood-supported elements. Our screen was trained only on concrete, masonry and render, so both laws are out of scope.
- San Francisco runs a facade program under SFEBC Chapter 5F [4]. A search on 2026-09-26 found no City of LA periodic facade ordinance. That only means we did not find one. It does not prove none exists.
- Public crack data, all CC BY 4.0:
  - Özgenel [5] is concrete crack tiles. Its filenames carry no source-photo IDs, so we cannot split it into train and test without tiles from the same photo landing on both sides (leakage). We used it for training only (`eval/facade/manifest_summary.json`).
  - SDNET2018 walls [6] name the source photo in each filename, so we could split it by photo.
  - BFDD [7] is real drone facade photos with pixel masks. We used it only as the out-of-domain (OOD) test, meaning data unlike the training set. Its archive has no README. We inferred that label value 1 means crack. The dataset does not document this.
- Why we trained our own model instead of taking a Hugging Face checkpoint:
  - dima806 [8] self-reports 0.9991 accuracy on 8,000 test images. Its card links a Kaggle notebook but does not name the dataset. Our verifier's guess, which it could not confirm, is that the data is a re-host of Özgenel. If so, dima806 is close to our own Özgenel-only model and is not an independent check.
  - The OpenSistemas YOLOv8 crack model [9] is AGPL-3.0 (copyleft).
  - crackevo [10] was trained on lab fracture images plus public crack sets. It reports pixel IoU 0.503±0.078 and says "no guarantee for field inspection use".
  - None of their cards shows a facade test, and we did not score any of them. The timm ResNet-18 backbone we fine-tuned is tagged Apache-2.0 [11].
- What each interior test produces:
  - Moisture meters give one number on a scale that is specific to the meter [12].
  - IR thermography gives thermograms and temperature differences, not moisture content [13][14].
  - RH probes give time series [15]. EPA says to keep indoor RH below 60% [16].
  - Rebound hammer and UPV give a rebound number (RN) and a pulse velocity (Vp) that can be paired with core strength [17][18][19].
  - Ground-penetrating radar (GPR), impact echo and sounding give radargrams, frequency spectra and marked areas [14][20].
  - Mold sampling is usually unnecessary when growth is visible [21].
- Only rebound and UPV have a large public dataset labelled with measured strength. The Matthews et al. database (CC BY 4.0) [17] holds 16,531 results from 115 studies. After cleaning we used 3,299 SonReb rows (rebound and UPV measured together) from 53 studies (`eval/interior/ndt_strength_v1.json`). The only public RH set we found (Tampere) is timber walls in a lab [15]. The BAM round-robin cores (CC0) [18][19] are not in the Matthews database, so we used them as an external test.

**What we built:**
- **Exterior screen** (`scripts/facade/*.py`, `src/cascade/facade/`, `models/facade/tilecls_resnet18_v1.onnx`, card `models/facade/tilecls_v1.card.json`):
  - Training: ResNet-18 fine-tuned for 4 epochs on 51,087 tiles: 40,000 from Özgenel plus 11,087 from the SDNET training photos (`eval/facade/tilecls_v1.json`, `training.main`).
  - SDNET split: we split the 72 source photos by photo, 44 for training, 14 for validation and 14 for test.
  - Threshold: we picked the highest threshold that keeps validation recall at or above 0.90. That gives p = 0.2050 (card).
  - On the page, `heatmap.py` cuts the photo into 224 px squares, scores each on CPU and shades the photo. The top squares can then go to three places:
    - the grader from the previous build, `cascade.grade.grade_image(asset_class="facade_element")`, which returns a FISP-style class. This runs only when an API key is set and the user ticks a confirmation box before crops are sent to the Anthropic API.
    - `cascade.measure.measure_crack`, which gives a width in mm only when the user supplies a scale (a mm-per-pixel value or a reference length on the photo).
    - a human review note, which always starts as "pending human review".
- **Interior estimator** (`src/cascade/interior/ndt.py`, `models/interior/ndt_strength_v1.json`):
  - It is a SonReb power law, ln fc = −8.755 + 0.858 ln RN + 1.085 ln Vp (`eval/interior/ndt_strength_v1.json`, `shipped.coefficients`), fitted by least squares.
  - It gives a split-conformal 90% range. That is a range built from errors on held-out studies, meant to contain the true value 90% of the time. The estimate can be shifted using a few cores from the same building.
  - If only one instrument was used, it falls back to a rebound-only or UPV-only law.
- **Field readings:** `readings.py` defines the `FieldReading` schema. The CSV template is `data/interior/field_readings_template.csv`. It has 33 columns covering location, method, instrument, scale, dry reference, RN and impact count, UPV path length and transit time, and core strength.
  - Rebound and UPV rows go to the estimator.
  - RH, leak-sensor and wood moisture readings are graded by rules.
  - Pin and pinless readings on gypsum, concrete or plaster return U (cannot be graded) because no dry-reference rule exists yet.
- **Test-methods table** (`eval/interior/methods_table.json`, shown on the Interior walls page):

Where the models lost or are weak:
- **The validation target was missed on test photos.** Test recall was 0.839 against the 0.90 we set on validation photos. With the shipped ONNX file, even validation recall at the card threshold is 0.8995 (`eval/facade/onnx_rescore.json`).
- **There are many false alarms on close-range photos.** Only 59.2% of flagged SDNET tiles are real cracks.
- **Little is saved on drone photos.** The screen skips only 13.8% of BFDD windows, and 87% of those windows are labelled crack anyway. The median BFDD frame has 9 windows, and the median number passed on is also 9 (`bfdd_frames` in `eval/facade/tilecls_v1.json`).
- **The old heuristic fails on drone photos.** Its AUROC on BFDD is 0.445, with a confidence interval up to 0.504. That is no better than a constant guess (0.500).
- **HGB lost to the power law.** On held-out studies the boosted-tree model (HistGradientBoosting, HGB) did worse than the power law. It only "wins" under random-row CV, which lets rows from the same study appear in both training and test.
- **All external cores are under-predicted.** On BAM the global model under-estimates every one of the 20 cores. Their strength spans only 40.3–61.6 MPa. The narrow range plus the constant under-estimate gives the negative R².
- **In-situ coverage is slightly under target:** 0.879 against 0.90.
- **Fallback numbers are not comparable with the SonReb numbers.** They are scored on different databases.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- **Pitch the facade screen as "look here first" for a QEWI.** It beats the old heuristic and the Özgenel-only model on AUROC and AP on both test sets. It does not replace a close-up inspection [1].
- **In-domain photos drove most of the gain.** Adding 11,087 SDNET wall tiles to training raised held-out AUROC from 0.749 to 0.918 with the same architecture. That run also used rebalanced class weights, and we did not try other model sizes.
- **The rebound + UPV strength estimate needs cores before any decision.** Three same-study specimens cut the in-situ error from 9.79 to 5.47 MPa. BAM shows the same pattern: 9.92 MPa uncalibrated, 4.43 MPa with 3 cores.
- **How we split the data changed the verdict.** Matthews et al. report R² 0.947 from 10-fold CV and do not say they grouped by study [22]. Compare that figure only with our random-row figure, never with the leave-study-out numbers. Even on random rows, our HGB, which uses only RN and Vp, reaches 0.638.

**Limits and what NOT to claim in the pitch:**
- **The grader's accuracy is unmeasured.** There is no public labelled set of FISP Safe / SWARMP / Unsafe photos (`src/cascade/evalmetrics.py`, `TRUTH_SOURCE["facade_element"]`). The page calls the class a suggestion. Do not quote any grader accuracy.
- **Do not say we beat Hugging Face models.** We scored none of them, and we dropped the dima806 comparison.
- **Do not claim coverage of SB 721/326 balconies.** Those elements rely on wood, and the screen never saw wood.
- **Do not claim drone performance in general.** BFDD is one published dataset, which we grouped into 10 flights, and its label map is inferred.
- **Width in mm needs a scale supplied by the user.** It comes from the old heuristic mask, whose error on facades has never been measured. The screen's squares are a fixed 224 pixels whatever the camera distance. There is no rescaling by GSD (ground sampling distance, how many mm one pixel covers).
- **The strength estimate is not a structural verdict.** The calibration test used labelled same-study specimens as a stand-in for cores. BAM rebound and UPV were measured on cores in labs, not on the structure.
- **No ML moisture model ships.** Most pin and pinless wall readings return U.
- **Speed was measured on a shared, loaded laptop.**

**Next steps:**
- Add a dry-reference `surface_moisture` rule family to `src/cascade/rubrics/interior_water.json`, so meter readings on gypsum, concrete and plaster stop returning U.
- Have a QEWI label a small set of facade photos so we can measure the grader.
- Score the dima806 checkpoint zero-shot on the same held-out rows before anyone compares us to it.
- Get photos and cores from a pilot building to test on local conditions, and add GSD rescaling.
- Re-measure CPU speed on an idle machine.

## 4. Common-area energy: smart switching and year-round plan

**Question (verbatim from the team lead):**
> get a ml model developed for Energy consumption smart switching for common areas and how we can build the mitigation for the needs year around needs.

**Short answer:** We built a lighting-switching policy for corridors, stairs, lobbies, garages, restrooms and amenity rooms. We also built three ML models and a 12-month plan for Los Angeles. The fire and energy codes shape most of the design. The code-required motion sensors do the switching. In the recommended mode the ML can only keep lights on longer, and it never switches anything off. The ML models mostly tie simple time-of-day schedules. In our semi-synthetic 32-floor tower, motion sensors alone save more energy (34.0%) than sensors plus ML (30.7%). In simulation, stair lighting design and sensor health mattered more for safety than the ML did. We expect the calendar actions to be worth more than the classifier, but we have not measured that.

**What we found (research):**
- Title 24 Part 6 (2022) section 130.1 sets these rules [1]:
  - Corridors and stairwells cut lighting power by at least 50% when empty.
  - Garages need a control step between 20% and 50% of design power, in zones of 500 W or less.
  - Restrooms go fully off within 20 min.
  - Up to 0.1 W/ft2 may stay on all the time in exit areas.
- The 2025 code (in force from 2026-01-01) keeps the corridor, stair and garage rules. Demand-responsive lighting (a cut of at least 15%) applies to projects with 4,000 W or more of general lighting [2]. Those controls must be certified to OpenADR 2.0b [3].
- ASHRAE 90.1-2019 asks for partial-off of at least 50% within 20 min. Garages must cut at least 50% after 10 min with no activity, in zones of 3,600 ft2 or less [4].
- These life-safety limits are the ones software must never cross [5][6]:
  - Exit routes stay at 1 fc (11 lux) or more whenever the building is occupied.
  - New stairs get 10 fc while someone is on them.
  - Motion switches on exit routes are allowed only if they fail to ON, hold for at least 15 min and react to any movement.
  - Emergency lighting lasts 90 min.
  - UL 924 relays force emergency lights on at power loss, whatever the controls do [7].
  - Caveat: we took these values from secondary sources, not the primary code text.
- LADWP pricing: high peak is Mon-Fri 13:00-16:59, and the high season is June-September [8]. On A-2 Rate B, the summer high-peak demand charge is $10.00/kW, against $4.75/kW in winter [9].
- LADWP demand response (DR), 2025 season terms: it runs Jun 15 to Oct 15. Events last 4 hours inside 13:00-21:00, with at most 20 per season. A building must cut at least 100 kW to take part [11].
- SCE summer on-peak runs 16:00-21:00 [12]. CAISO Flex Alerts usually run 16:00-21:00 [13].
- We found no public dataset that records occupancy in high-rise corridors or stairs. The closest real data we found:
  - ROBOD: 5 university rooms in Singapore, with presence and recorded lighting kWh but no motion-sensor column [14].
  - UCI 357: one office in 2015. Its light-sensor column leaks the answer [15].
  - UCI 864: of the datasets we checked, the only one with real motion-sensor (PIR) readings next to ground truth. It covers 4 days [16].
  - BDG2: 24 parking buildings. 20 meters pass our 80% data-coverage filter, and only 2 of those are on Pacific time [17].
- Published results on savings:
  - LBNL's meta-analysis puts occupancy controls at 24% savings on average, and 38% with several strategies combined [21].
  - PNNL garage results ranged from 76% extra savings to almost none, depending on commissioning [22].

**What we built:**

All the code is in `src/cascade/building/energy/` and is run by `scripts/train_energy_models.py`.

- **`common_area_rules.json` and `rules.py`**: a rule table for each zone type, with its code basis and a source tag. It holds the life-safety floors: 1 fc on exit routes, 10 fc on stairs in use, a fail-safe hold of at least 15 min, full ON when a sensor fails, 90-min emergency lighting, and UL 924 kept outside AI control. A copy is in `eval/energy/rules.json`.
- **`policy.py`**: a fixed set of rules (a state machine), with light level = max(code floor, target).
  - In the recommended mode, "sensor + ML, extend-only", the ML can keep a zone lit only while the sensor hold is already running. It cannot switch an empty zone on or any zone off.
  - A sensor fault means full ON.
  - DR trims never touch exit routes.
  - Every change is a proposal marked `pending_approval` until a named person approves or rejects it.
- **`occupancy.py`**:
  - M1 answers "is the space occupied now?" It is a gradient-boosting model on CO2, sound, VOC, humidity, temperature, Wi-Fi device count and time. Lighting, lux, plug-load and HVAC readings are left out on purpose, because they would leak the answer.
  - M2 answers "occupied in 1 hour?" It has a deployable version (fed by M1) and an oracle version (fed by true presence).
- **`forecast.py`**: M3 forecasts tomorrow's garage load, issued at 00:00. It is compared with two naive forecasts: the same hour yesterday and the same hour last week. It is scored with CV(RMSE) [24]. A separate comparison with Chronos-Bolt, a pretrained forecasting model used without any training on our data [23], runs from `scripts/train_energy_models.py chronos` in a separate torch environment.
- **`simulate.py`** runs two tests:
  - (A) A replay on ROBOD test days. Policy kWh = recorded kWh x light level, so a policy can only remove energy the room really used. Motion-sensor misses are added at the UCI 864 rate.
  - (B) A 2025 tower year. The building is an assumed 32-floor inventory (`inventory.py`). It uses resampled ROBOD test days and simulated demand-response days.
- **`tariff.py` and `calendar_la.py`**: the LADWP, SCE, DR and Flex Alert tables, and the 12-month calendar. The calendar uses NOAA normals [18][25], NOAA solar equations [19] and Open-Meteo 2025 reanalysis [20].
- **Where to see it:** the **"Common-area energy"** page (`streamlit run app/site.py`, page file `app/site_pages/energy.py`). It shows:
  - the tower tiles and a stair caveat
  - the policy comparison on real rooms, with the share of occupied time left under-lit
  - a what-if for degraded sensors
  - the model card with its baselines
  - the garage forecast
  - the calendar heatmap and "What the building manager does each season"
  - the approval queue

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- **Keep ML out of the decision to switch off.** ML-only switching left 2.5% to 23.6% of occupied time under-lit in the real rooms. In the tower it left stairs under 10 fc for 101,965 minutes a year.
- **Make code motion sensors the default and the ML extension optional.** In the tower, the extension costs 19,139 kWh a year. Its safety gain is small (420 to 400 stair minutes). In the real-room replay on training days it showed no gain at all (under-lit time 0.0024% with or without it).
- **Lighting design does more for stair safety than ML.** Sensors alone with 20 fc stairs gave 0 stair minutes and 31.4% savings in simulation. That beats sensor + ML at the current design (400 min, 30.7%) on both counts. The extra wattage is an assumption, so a lighting designer must confirm it with a photometric check.
- **In our simulation, sensor health is the biggest lever.** At a 10% miss rate, stair shortfall rises from 420 to 8,425 minutes. Fund commissioning and fault detection before more ML.
- **Consider a pretrained forecaster for garage load.** Our M3 model barely beats yesterday's load (0.091 vs 0.096). Chronos-Bolt did better, but rerun it first (see Limits).
- **Pitch the year-round plan as approvable rules, not as measured savings.** Only two calendar items are quantified: the 2.77 kW peak cut and the 1.48 kW DR trim.

**Limits and what NOT to claim in the pitch:**
- **Do not say "the AI saves 30.7%".** The sensors and code rules save it. The AI part costs 19,139 kWh a year of it.
- **Do not say "never breaks a life-safety floor".**
  - The 1 fc exit-route result is 0 by construction, so it proves nothing.
  - In the simulation, stairs were below 10 fc for 400 min a year.
  - The builder's first summary said "without breaking a safety floor". Do not reuse it. (`docs/research/13_common_area_energy.md` conclusion 2 has since been corrected and now states the 400 stair minutes.)
- **Do not say "our occupancy model beats schedules".** It is clearly better in 1 of 5 rooms and loses badly on the unseen library room (0.650 vs 0.906).
- **Tower kWh and $ are not measured.** The tower rests on these assumptions:
  - Areas, W/ft2 and design fc are assumed.
  - Each garage level is one control zone, but the code wants zones of 500 W or less.
  - DST hour shifts are ignored, and HVAC is not simulated.
  - Weekends reuse weekday days.
  - All 64 stairwells replay the same 9 December test days of one lecture room, which was 2.1% occupied.
- **The replay's "motion sensor" is simulated.** It is true presence plus independent misses at a rate from a 4-day lab dataset. That flatters sensors, because real failures come in clusters. The home-page card's "motion sensors ... on real rooms" figure is this same simulated sensor.
- **ROBOD is not a high-rise.** It is 5 university rooms in Singapore, and how its ground truth was collected is not documented.
- **The garage meters are not LA lighting circuits.** They are whole-garage loads from 2016-2017, and only 2 of the 20 meters are on Pacific time.
- **The $ figures understate a real bill.** They use LADWP base charges only (adjustment factors excluded) [10]. Demand is a coincident-peak estimate, and SCE prices are not modelled.
- **The Chronos result needs a rerun before anyone quotes it.**
  - The 0.074 result file (`eval/energy/garage_m3_chronos.json`) was written before a fix for a small look-ahead: the reviewer counted 23 of 7,300 forecast days where test-day values leaked into the input.
  - The file lacks the `context_fill` field that the fixed script writes. A rerun was started but did not finish, so the file is unchanged.
  - We did not check whether BDG2 was in the model's pretraining data.
- **Do not claim DR revenue.** 1.48 kW is far below LADWP's 100 kW minimum.
- **Do not claim code compliance.** The NFPA 101 and CFC values come from secondary sources. An engineer and the local code authority must confirm settings.
- **The recommended rule was chosen after seeing the test days.** The training-day check does not confirm its safety benefit.
- **Nothing actuates equipment.** Approvals on the page last for the browser session only. The append-only log exists in `policy.py`, but the page does not write to it.

**Next steps:**
- Rerun `scripts/train_energy_models.py chronos` in the torch environment, then update the Chronos numbers.
- In `docs/research/13_common_area_energy.md` conclusion 2, soften "the price of a small safety margin", since the training-day check showed no safety gain. (Conclusions 2 and 4 already carry the stair and "hypothesis, not measured" corrections.)

The money is in the **$10/kW summer high-peak demand charge** and in **demand-response payments** (LADWP commercial DR, Jun 15-Oct 15, 13:00-21:00 window, pays $10-15/kW-month plus $0.25/kWh during events, **minimum 100 kW curtailment**; all from `tariff.json`).
- **Illustration** (assumptions: a 100 kW / 400 kWh battery, charged at base and discharged 13:00-17:00 on about 88 summer weekdays, 85% round-trip efficiency, which is our assumption): energy gap about **$770/yr**; demand charge about **$4,000/yr** (100 kW x $10 x 4 months) only if it holds the peak down every day, since one miss sets the month's demand; DR payments possible if a battery discharge counts as curtailment (not checked). We have **no installed-cost figure**, so payback is unknown.
- **Lighting alone is too small.** The simulated tower's common-area peak cut is 2.8 kW and its mean DR trim is 1.5 kW (`eval/energy/tower_year.json`), far below the 100 kW DR minimum. Load shifting pays at whole-building scale: chillers and pre-cooling, garage EV charging, pumps.
- **Cheaper than batteries:** pre-cooling the building in the morning, ice or chilled-water storage, and moving flexible loads to base hours. Our calendar proposes these, but HVAC is not simulated, so there are no numbers yet.
- **Pitch angle (roadmap):** a battery room adds fire risk (NFPA 855 governs stationary storage). Cerebro could both schedule storage and watch battery health: storage plus safety in one product.
- **Next step:** a load-shift simulator on the energy page (tower load profile, LADWP rates, sliders for battery size and round-trip efficiency), labelled SIMULATED.

## 5. LA rain direction and which side of a building gets wet

**Question (verbatim from the team lead):**
> Find the rain coverage patterns for LA county and which side of the building will have more water impact because of the rain direction find if there is any co-relation and how can we build a AI model for that.(generalise for LA area, research findings can be taken from anywhere)

**Short answer:** In the LA basin and the San Fernando Valley, walls facing east and south-east get the most wind-driven rain. That held at all 7 airport stations there (`eval/rain/summary.json`, `n_basin_E_SE` 7 of 7). On the coast and in the basin, the everyday wind blows from the west or south-west, but storm rain comes from the east or south-east. So at those stations a normal wind rose points at the wrong wall. The link between rain and wind direction is clear at all 12 stations. It is strongest in Downtown LA and weakest in Ontario. We built an AI model. On average it has less error per wall than simple rules, but it is not better at naming the single wettest wall, and on real forecasts it is not clearly better. Our most solid result is the measured station averages. They show relative exposure at airports. We have not checked them against leaks in real buildings.

**What we found (research):**
- ISO 15927-3 method 1 gives a "driving-rain index" for each wall from hourly wind speed, wind direction and rain. The hourly formula is (2/9) × U10 × Rh^(8/9) × cos(D − θ). It counts only the hours when the wind blows onto the wall. Wind speed is raised to the power 1 [1]. The research pilot used the power 8/9 by mistake. The verifier caught it, and the built code uses the power 1 (`iso_hourly` in `src/cascade/building/rainexposure.py`). We read the ISO standard only through [1]. iso.org returned HTTP 403 [2].
- The index for a real wall multiplies that value by a height factor C_R, a topography factor C_T, an obstruction factor O and a wall factor W [1].
- A standards shop page lists the scope limits of ISO 15927-3. The method does not cover mountain areas with sheer cliffs or deep gorges. It also does not cover places where more than 25% of the yearly rain comes from severe convective storms [3].
- On a building, the windward wall gets wettest at the top corners, then along the top and side edges [4][5]. Measured catch coefficients on real buildings range from 0.02 to 0.26 s/m. So we need to calibrate on site before we can quote litres on a wall [1].
- In southern California, 10 to 40 hours of rain make up more than half of the yearly total. One storm can bring 10-30% of the year's rain [6].
- ASOS airport wind is a 2-minute average, reported to the nearest 10°. Winds of 2 kt or less are reported as calm [8]. The ISO method works from hourly data [1].
- The Downtown LA station (KCQT) moved on 2024-05-20. The station that replaced it sends no METAR reports [9].
- Data used: IEM ASOS hourly reports, which are public domain [7]; NCEI 1991-2020 normals and GHCN-Daily [10]; ERA5 reanalysis through Open-Meteo [11]; and GFS day-1 forecasts through the Open-Meteo Previous Runs API [12]. Open-Meteo's free tier is for non-commercial use only [13].

**What we built:**
- `scripts/fetch_la_rain_data.py` downloads and caches all the data under `data/raw/rain/`, which git ignores.
  - It keeps a full manifest in `data/raw/rain/sources_manifest.json`. This copy is local only. It has a sha256 for each of the 65 files.
  - It writes a slim copy to `eval/rain/sources_manifest.json`. That copy has the URL, request parameters, access date, licence, origin, rows and bytes, but no sha256.
  - 24 of the 65 files were copied from the research pilot's cache. The pilot downloaded them with the same parameters, and the manifest marks those files.
- `src/cascade/building/rainexposure.py` holds four things:
  - the ISO physics (`iso_hourly`, `c_r`, `obstruction_factor`, `wall_index`);
  - data checks against GHCN-Daily (`qc_daily`, `qc_water_years`);
  - circular statistics (a Rayleigh test and a block permutation test);
  - a building API: `facade_exposure`, `exposure_rank`, `exposure_shares` and `nearest_station`.
- `src/cascade/building/rainmodel.py` builds the storm tables and the model.
  - Storms are found from ERA5 (or GFS) rain, so false alarms count against the model.
  - The model is 8 gradient-boosted regressors (sklearn HistGradientBoostingRegressor), one per wall direction, trained on log1p of the target.
  - It includes three baselines:
    - B0 is the station's average storm value for each wall in the training years.
    - B1 is the ISO physics applied to ERA5 10 m wind, with no learning.
    - B2 is a least-squares coefficient per station and per wall, times ERA5 storm rain. It is pooled across stations for a station the model has not seen.
  - Confidence intervals come from a storm-cluster bootstrap. A cluster is a group of storms that start within 36 h of each other across stations.
- `scripts/build_la_rain.py` writes the output files: `eval/rain/*.json`, the parquet files, `models/rain/hgb_era5_full.joblib` (10 m and 100 m ERA5 features) and `models/rain/hgb_10m.joblib` (10 m features only).
- **Page:** run `streamlit run app/site.py` and open **Protect → "Rain exposure (LA)"** (`app/site_pages/rain_la.py`). The page shows:
  - a station map (REAL) with modelled ERA5 grid cells (MODELLED);
  - the rain rose next to the all-hours wind rose;
  - bars per wall, a strip by water year, and a heatmap of all 12 stations by 8 walls;
  - the statistics and the model evaluation;
  - a "Check your building" tool. You enter a station or coordinates, the direction a wall faces, the height, the terrain and a nearby building. It returns an inspection order for four walls, marked ADVISORY.
- **Tests:** the builder reported 29 tests passing in the workflow journal. The unit tests use SYNTHETIC fixtures, plus an artifact self-consistency test and a page check (`docs/research/14_la_rain_facade_exposure.md`). I did not re-run them, because this pass was read-only.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- **Rule of thumb for LA buildings (relative exposure measured at airports):**
  - In the basin and the San Fernando Valley, inspect and seal the E and SE walls first.
  - Near Fullerton, start with SE. Near Santa Ana, start with the S wall.
  - In the Antelope Valley, start with W or SW.
  - In Ontario, the W signal is weak, so check all sides.
  - Higher floors get more rain (ISO height factor C_R [1]). The top corners and edges of the windward wall get the most [4][5].
  - At the basin and valley stations, 88.7-89.5% of the driving-rain index falls from November to March. Inspect before November.
- **The correlation is real, and it is about direction, not just amount.** On the coast and in the basin (LAX, SMO, HHR, CQT, LGB), rain comes from roughly the opposite side to the everyday wind. In the San Fernando Valley the everyday wind already comes from the south or south-east (BUR 158.9°, VNY 126.9°), so the gap there is smaller. In the Antelope Valley, rain and everyday wind come from about the same direction (PMD 239.1° vs 239.2°), so a normal wind rose works there.
- **How to build the AI model:** feed the ISO physics in as the core input, and learn a correction from measured station data. That is the design we have working. Lead with the measured station averages. Present the model as a modest helper that proposes an inspection order for a storm, and have a person approve any work.

**Limits and what NOT to claim in the pitch:**
- Do not say "the AI predicts which wall a storm will hit hardest." In the four main tests it ties or loses to a simple rule on the wettest wall. There is no confidence interval for that metric. Two side runs did better on top-1: the 10 m model on later years scored 0.462 vs B0 0.428 (`metrics.json` `temporal_10m`), and the same-period ERA5 reference scored 0.532 vs B1 0.387 (`forecast_period_era5_reference`). Neither run used real forecasts.
- Do not say the model beats forecasts. On real GFS forecasts its confidence interval against B2 crosses zero.
- Do not quote litres on a wall. The values are relative exposure. C_T and W are set to 1, and the measurements come from airports at 10 m, not from buildings.
- Do not claim the model covers upper floors, street canyons or tower effects. The building tool applies only the ISO height factor C_R and the obstruction factor O. None of our data resolves wind above 100 m.
- We have not checked exposure against real leak or damage records, so do not claim it predicts damage.
- Do not quote the research pilot numbers, such as "13.3x" or "p 1.7e-8 to 2.8e-48". The pilot used the wrong wind exponent, a 12 h storm gap and no data checks.
- Map cells are MODELLED, with expected skill equal to the leave-one-station-out scores. 8 cells sit more than 50 m above the highest training station (774 m). They are outside the training range and may fall outside ISO's scope [3]. LAX/SMO share one ERA5 cell and CQT/HHR share another, so ERA5 cannot tell those pairs apart.
- In the leave-one-station-out test, the same storms are seen at other stations during training. The test that holds out both station and years avoids this, and there the model loses on the wettest wall.
- Downtown (CQT) is calm in 0.646 of all hours. Its direction shares describe only the hours with wind.
- Open-Meteo data is for non-commercial use on the free tier [13].

## 6. Fire de-escalation plan with AI (and the drawings)

**Question (verbatim from the team lead):**
> how can we have a fire descalation plans with AI research about that and draw something useful

**Short answer:** Yes for the plan and the drawings. No for an AI fire detector. We built a fixed-rule plan card that works for any room, core, shaft or electrical room of our SYNTHETIC 32-floor tower. We also made three drawings with code. They show who moves where and who decides. We also trained a model on REAL lab sensor data to tell a real fire from a nuisance. It failed in a room it had never seen. It called all 11 nuisance episodes fire-like, the same as the simple trigger. **The pitch should lead with the plan card and the drawings, not the detector.**

**What we found (research):**
- The building's listed fire alarm system acts first, and we do not touch it. The voice alarm must reach at least the alarming floor, the floor above and the floor below [2]. The LAFD high-rise sequence policy lists the automatic actions [1]. Our fact check corrected one claim. Passenger elevators recall automatically only when an elevator lobby or hoistway detector goes off. Pull stations, area smoke detectors, duct detectors and waterflow do not recall them (LAFD matrix, footnote g) [1]. The firefighter's smoke control panel has "the highest priority of any control point" [3]. Every required interior exit stair has a standpipe hose connection at every floor landing [4]. In high-rises, every floor has a supervised sprinkler floor control valve [5].
- A person, the Fire Safety Director (FSD), runs the building side. In LA the FSD calls 911 (or assigns someone to call) and directs the evacuation. Yearly drills relocate occupants "to a predetermined floor or outside safe refuge area". Towers of 35 or more stories also run a full evacuation drill every three years [6].
- Partial relocation is the normal plan. NFPA's high-rise guide says limited evacuations (the fire floors plus the floors above and below) "have proved effective". It also asks for a pre-incident building card [7]. NIST TN 1664 studied a real fire in a 32-story office building. People were confused when a "stay on your floor" message was followed by "evacuate three floors down" [8]. So the message has to be simple and consistent.
- Our fire department tactics come from a Sacramento regional guideline, not from an LAFD procedure. Stage at least two floors below the fire. Put equipment outside the attack stair, one floor below the fire. Crews leave the elevator at least two floors below the fire. The guideline authorizes elevator use for fires above the sixth floor [9].
- Stack effect can fill stairwells and elevator shafts with smoke [10].
- First Interstate Bank, Los Angeles, 1988. The fire started on floor 12 of a 62-story tower and burned floors 12 to 16. The sprinklers were about 90 % installed but not in service. The standpipe had been shut off. A maintenance worker took a service elevator to floor 12 to check the alarm and died. About 50 people were above the fire, and five were rescued from the roof by helicopter [11][12]. We took three rules from it: show impairments first, check alarms remotely and never by elevator, and plan for the fire spreading upward.
- Battery rooms: in the 2019 energy-storage explosion in Surprise, Arizona, four firefighters were hurt after a door was opened. The report recommends remote gas monitoring [13].
- AI research: NIST P-Flash predicts flashover and was trained on simulations [14][15]. Video detection and sensor-fusion detection exist [16][17]. Vorwerk et al. report up to 69 % classification (4 fire types) with no data from the target room [17]. Fire alarm control units are listed under UL 864, and an unlisted AI cannot replace listed detection [18]. For governance we follow the NIST AI RMF [19].
- Our conclusion from the research: **AI is read-only decision support.** It never silences, delays or resets an alarm. It never commands the smoke control panel, sprinklers, pumps, elevators, stair locks or HVAC. It never chooses the evacuation scope and never sends anyone toward the fire. A "nuisance-like" score never downgrades a code alarm (`src/cascade/building/fire_rules.json`, `ai_never`). Decision D-015 item 7 in `docs/decisions.md` records the read-only rule.

**What we built:**
- **Plan card** (`src/cascade/building/fire.py`, `plan_for_incident`). It reads every threshold from `src/cascade/building/fire_rules.json`. Each line shows its basis: either a cited code or guideline URL, or the tag [team-proposed, validate]. Six of the 17 thresholds carry that tag, and two of those six have no URL (`eval/fire/planner_checks.json`, `rules`). Every line has `executes=False`. Every decision names who approves it: the FSD or the fire department incident commander (IC). No ML is used. Example card for a fire in the east office of F19 (`eval/fire/plan_card_F19-E.md`, SYNTHETIC):
  - People: alert F18-F20. Relocate them down Stair A to F14-F16. The alert floors have a design load of 258 people, 86 per floor. That is a code design figure, not a headcount. Two people on those floors need help (a SYNTHETIC list).

## 7. Pipe clogging: testing, prevention and periodic checks

**Question (verbatim from the team lead):**
> how can we test the pipe-clogging and prevention from the flow and hardware periodic checking, with AI software research and build some conclusions from that

**Short answer:** In our simulation, just watching riser pressure did not reveal clogs. A short nightly test that draws a known flow at the top of each pressure zone did. At 4.0 L/s the test flagged 98.1% of severe-clog nights and 49.3% of moderate-clog nights, with false alarms on 3.9% of clean nights. It flagged only 6.2% of mild-clog nights, so mild clogs looked like clean pipe. Machine learning did not beat a plain rule at finding or locating clogs. It only graded severity better (macro-F1 0.584 vs 0.492). On one real sewer blockage (Bellinge, Denmark), checks against a learned "normal" level alarmed 20 and 70 minutes after onset, with no other alarms in 3.38 held-out months. That is one event, so it is a case study. Periodic checks start from LA rules. Everything else runs on a risk-based calendar that a person approves.

**What we found (research):**
- **Supply riser physics.** A partial clog adds a local loss h = K·v²/2g on top of pipe friction [1]. The loss grows with the square of velocity. At low night-time household flows, a clog is nearly invisible. WNTR 1.5.0 runs EPANET from Python and lets us set a loss coefficient K for each pipe [2].
- **Drain physics.** Drains are gravity pipes. They run partly full and get short bursts from fixtures. A blockage holds water upstream and starves the pipe downstream. In the real Bellinge event, the upstream daily mean depth rose from 0.080 m (22 July 2020) to 1.117 m (24 July). Downstream it fell from 0.048 m to 0.021 m (`eval/clog/bellinge_case.json`, `daily_means_m`). In stacks, bends of more than 45° cause a sudden velocity drop and a hydraulic jump [6]. From that we infer that stack bases and offsets are likely build-up points. The source does not say this directly. LA Sanitation says cooling grease hardens inside pipes and causes sewage spills [19].
- **Code limits.** Section 608.2 (UPC-based) says static pressure above 80 psi needs a pressure regulator (PRV) with a strainer in front of it. Regulators of 1-1/2 in (40 mm) or larger are exempt from the strainer rule [3]. So a strainer at a riser-size PRV is a design choice, not a code requirement. We did not check whether the California code says the same.
- **The drain limits the test flow.** A DN100 stack takes at most 4.0 L/s with square entries and 5.2 L/s with swept entries. This comes from EN 12056-2 / DIN 1986-100, as summarised by Lansing 2023 [4]. If the test water drains into such a stack, the test must stay at or below 4.0 L/s. Larger draws need a break tank.
- **Sensor noise.** One vendor's clamp-on transit-time flow meter quotes ±1% of reading from 0.5 to 12 m/s, and ±0.0046 m/s below 0.5 m/s [5]. We model whichever is larger. The pressure noise we simulated (sd 0.15 m per reading) is our own assumption. We did not check it against a datasheet (`eval/clog/riser_metrics.json` `design.noise`; `src/cascade/building/clog/rules.json` `PRESSURE_NOISE_M`).
- **Published methods.**
  - A learned "normal" level plus a control-chart alarm is an established way to catch sewer blockages. We read the abstract only [10].
  - Active acoustic drain tests exist, but only in the lab (abstract only) [11].
  - Pressure-wave (transient) tests for partial blockages have not yet been tried in large, complex, real systems (abstract only) [12].
  - Drain-camera AI is sold as "assisted". Certified people still confirm the defect codes [13].
  - Clog analytics are sold for city sewers [14].
  - The building water monitors we checked advertise leak checks, not clog tests. That covers only the pages we read [15, 16].
- **Real data.** We found no public building-drain dataset with labelled blockages. Bellinge is a municipal combined sewer (CC BY 4.0). It logs one throttle-pipe blockage, 23-28 July 2020 [8, 9]. HSB Living Lab (Sweden, CC BY 4.0) gives real apartment water use at 10-minute steps [7].
- **Periodic checks in LA.**
  - Backflow assemblies must be tested at least once a year by a tester licensed by LA County Public Health (LADWP Rule 16-D, sections 9.1-9.2) [17].
  - Grease interceptors at permitted food businesses must be cleaned before fats, oils and grease (FOG) plus solids reach 25% of the liquid depth. Grease traps are cleaned daily (LASAN SSMP v3.0, 7.5.3) [18].
  - LA Sanitation sets how often it cleans each city sewer from that pipe's history. It camera-inspects a pipe usually within 48 hours after an overflow [18]. This is city-sewer practice, not a building rule.
  - We found no official fixed interval for in-building drains.

**What we built:**
- **Riser simulation** (`src/cascade/building/clog/geometry.py`, `src/cascade/building/clog/sim_riser.py`, `scripts/clog_riser_sim.py`, `scripts/clog_sim_lib.py`).
  - A synthetic 32-floor tower with three pressure zones. PRVs feed the low and mid zones and the booster feeds the top zone. Every outlet is at or below 80 psi static.
  - Real HSB demand drives it: 652,315 rows from 28 apartments (`eval/clog/data_manifest.json`, `hsb_stats`).
  - Clogs are injected on 6 riser segments and 2 PRV strainers.
- **Detectors** (`src/cascade/building/clog/features.py`, `src/cascade/building/clog/detect.py`, `scripts/clog_riser_eval.py`, model `models/clog/riser_act4.joblib`).
  - Nightly active test at 4.0 or 2.5 L/s. The feature is the change in segment head loss divided by the change in flow squared, minus the value from commissioning.
  - Passive features over 24 hours.
  - A plain z-score rule compared with gradient boosting. A BMS low-pressure alarm is the baseline.
  - Alarm thresholds are set on out-of-fold clean training nights.
  - Train: 480 scenarios over 16 weeks. Test: 240 scenarios over 8 later weeks. The test uses a different apartment-to-unit map but the same riser.
- **Drain-down test** (`scripts/clog_drain_sim.py`, `src/cascade/building/clog/drain.py`).
  - A pyswmm model of a building drain [20], with the clog modelled as a partly closed orifice.
  - The test sends 2.5 L/s for 60 s (150 L). It times how long the level takes to fall back and compares that with the commissioning median (89.5 s).
- **Bellinge case** (`scripts/clog_bellinge.py`, `src/cascade/building/clog/bellinge.py`).
  - A gradient-boosting model learns "normal" upstream depth from the downstream gauge.
  - A paired rule alarms when upstream is above normal AND downstream is below 70% of its 7-day median.
  - Onset is 2020-07-23 18:30: the first 5-minute step where upstream depth is more than 0.10 m above its 24-hour median.
- **Check calendar** (`src/cascade/building/clog/schedule.py`, `src/cascade/building/clog/rules.json`, `scripts/clog_schedule.py`). Every rule is tagged PUBLIC, team-proposed or Assumption.
- **Page:** "Clog Watch (pipes)", in the Operate group of `app/site.py`. The file is `app/site_pages/pipes.py`.
  - It shows a riser sensor diagram and three results tabs (Supply riser, Drains, Real blockage). It also shows the check calendar, conclusions and data provenance.
  - Every calendar row carries "requires human approval".
  - A facilities person approves the nightly test schedule once. The building management system (BMS) runs it and stops it on low pressure. Cerebro never opens a valve itself.
  - Tests use domestic cold water only, never fire, sprinkler or standpipe piping.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- **Test at a known flow. Do not just listen.**
  - In simulation, only the nightly active test flagged supply clogs clearly above its own false-alarm rate.
  - Passive detectors and the BMS alarm flagged severe clogs at about their false-alarm rate. Passive gradient boosting: 4.6% vs 4.5%. Passive z-score: 8.8% vs 8.6%. BMS: 1.8% vs 1.8%.
  - Use the highest flow the drain can take: 4.0 L/s into a DN100 stack with square entries, or more into a break tank.
  - The 2.5 L/s test was clearly weaker. It caught 31.5% of moderate clogs with 17.9% false alarms.
- **Machine learning adds grading, not detection.**
  - Detection: gradient boosting caught fewer moderate clogs than the z-score rule (30.0% vs 49.3%), with fewer false alarms (0.6% vs 3.9%). The intervals overlap. It did not win.
  - Under the 2-in-3-nights work-order rule it flagged 44.9% of moderate weeks, against 69.4% for the rule.
  - Locating: about the same (73.2% vs 70.3%, overlapping intervals).
  - Grading: gradient boosting scored higher (macro-F1 0.584 vs 0.492). But it called 221 of 336 clean test nights "mild". The rule called 407 of 434 mild nights "clean" (confusion matrices, `riser_metrics.json`).
  - Recommendation: detect with the rule. Use ML only to suggest a severity grade, and have a person check it.
- **Where ML lost:**
  - Passive gradient boosting graded below a random guess (0.222 vs 0.248).
  - In the busier-tower stress test, passive gradient boosting's false alarms rose to 13.1%. The passive z-score rule stayed at 5.4%.
  - At 2.5 L/s, gradient boosting had more false alarms than the rule (24.7% vs 17.9%). It also caught more moderate clogs (38.5% vs 31.5%). Neither came close to the 5% target.
  - Where ML did better: with doubled sensor noise, gradient boosting kept false alarms lower than the rule (23.8% vs 54.2%). Both were far above target.
- **Drains: time the drain-down. Do not just watch the peak level.**
  - In simulation, drain-down time flagged every test when the clog left 20% or less of the pipe open. At 25% open it flagged 71.7%.
  - Peak level flagged every test only at 12% open or less. At 20% open it flagged 20.0%.
  - Neither could tell a clog that leaves half the pipe open from a clean drain.
- **Real data: a learned normal level cut false alarms at Bellinge.**
  - Both checks built on the learned normal had 0 other held-out alarms.
  - Residual-only was the fastest detector (20 min, vs 35 min for a fixed 1.0 m level). It had 8 alarm episodes in the training months.
  - The paired rule had no other episodes anywhere. It was slower (70 min) than the fixed 1.0 m alarm, which raised 3 other held-out alarms.
  - This is one event. It supports a claim about false alarms, not a claim about speed for the paired rule.
- **Where to put sensors.** This is our design. We tested it only in simulation.
  - Supply side: a clamp-on flow meter on each zone header.
  - Pressure sensors at the booster, at each PRV strainer's inlet and outlet, and at the bottom, middle and top floor of each zone.
  - One test valve at the top of each zone.
  - Drain side: a level sensor at each stack base and main cleanout, a FOG probe in the grease interceptor, and a downstream partner gauge wherever a pair is possible.
  - The mid-zone strainer was the hardest place to detect (63.8%). Where a strainer is fitted, measure its pressure drop directly.
- **Periodic checks.**
  - Legal maximums come first: backflow every 365 days, grease interceptors before FOG plus solids reach 25%, grease traps daily.
  - Supply risers get the nightly test. A work order is proposed after 2 alarms in 3 nights.
  - Stack and drain camera checks start from a team-proposed 365-day base. It is shortened for the number of floors served, past backups and drain-down results.
  - Strainers start from a team-proposed 180-day base.
  - Recommission after any pipe or sensor work.

**Limits and what NOT to claim in the pitch:**
- Do not say "AI detects clogs" or "prevents backups".
  - Every riser and drain number is SIMULATED with INJECTED clogs. Nothing has been tried in a real building.
  - Detection came from the plain rule, not from ML.
- Do not quote "98%" without saying "severe clogs, in simulation". Moderate was 49.3%. No method separated mild clogs from clean pipe.
- Do not say Cerebro runs tests or opens valves on its own. A person approves the schedule, and the BMS runs it.
- Several inputs are assumptions, not calibrated to real blockages: the pressure noise (sd 0.15 m), the K severity bands and the tower geometry.
  - The "held-out building" is the same 28 apartments on a different unit map, with the same riser.
  - The confidence intervals come from only 8 test weeks.
- The demand is Swedish apartment use only. The 39.3% of metered volume from shared spaces was dropped (`data_manifest.json`). The x2.5 busier-tower test stands in for an LA tower. It is not LA data.
- Thresholds were set for 5% false alarms. They did not carry over at 2.5 L/s (17.9% for the rule, 24.7% for gradient boosting).
- The test is proposed as a 60-120 s hold, but only the settled 10-minute flow step was simulated. Transients were not modelled.
  - A drifting sensor offset was not tested. The constant-offset row only checks the algebra.
  - One week fell back to WNTR's own solver. Which weeks fall back can differ between reruns.
- Bellinge is one event in a municipal combined sewer, not a building drain.
  - The paired-rule settings were chosen after the event was known.
  - Rain was not used, because the rain gauges are CC BY-NC. Some other alarms may be wet weather.
  - A sensor scaling change covers the whole 2020 window.
  - It is a case study, not an accuracy figure. Do not say the paired rule "detects earlier than a fixed alarm". It does not.
- The 48-hour camera rule and the 365-day and 180-day intervals are team-proposed, not LA rules for buildings.
  - The asset register is SYNTHETIC.
  - We have not checked whether the California plumbing code matches.

**Limits and what NOT to claim in the pitch:**
- **Do not say "runs fully offline" if we click the grader.** The exterior grader sends image crops to the Claude API when `.env` has an `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`. The facade fix report says this laptop has one. The button stays disabled until the confirmation box is ticked. The grader's accuracy on facades has not been measured [1][4].
- **Do not say "decisions are logged".** The home page says "the decision is logged" (`home.py:231`) and "safe step, logged" (`home.py:170`). The energy page keeps approve/reject only for the browser session (`energy.py:379-397`), and the page itself says so [3].
- **No customer building has been tested.** The home page says so (`home.py:203`). The energy tower is SEMI-SYNTHETIC, the fire tower is SYNTHETIC and the clog riser is SIMULATED [3][4].
- **Energy:**
  - Do not say "the AI saved 30%". The 30.70% is measured against an always-on building. Motion sensors alone save more, 33.97%, and the ML extension costs an extra 19,139 kWh and $1,116 a year (SEMI-SYNTHETIC, `eval/energy/summary.json`).
  - With people on the stairs, lighting is below 10 fc for 400 min/yr with ML and 420 min/yr with sensors only (SEMI-SYNTHETIC + INJECTED, same file).
  - The review's blocker and major issue were about these claims. `energy.py` now shows the stair and ML-cost tiles (lines 150-167), but no fix report or re-review confirms they are closed [4][10].
- **Numeric:**
  - The forecast does beat the baseline: Chronos-2 WAPE is 6.19% against 10.12% for seasonal naive, on 20 REAL office meters (`eval/numeric/backtest_bdg2.json`).
  - Do not call the 80% band calibrated. Its coverage is 0.730 for Chronos-2, 0.736 for HGB and 0.772 for Bolt (same file).
- **Facade:**
  - It is a screening tool, not a FISP finding [1].
  - On the REAL SDNET test set (3,529 tiles, 14 photos), recall is 0.839 and precision is 0.592 (`eval/facade/tilecls_v1.json`). The shipped ONNX file scores 0.837 and 0.593 (`eval/facade/onnx_rescore.json`).
  - It does not cover wood balconies under SB 721/326 [4].
- **Fire:**
  - By design, the AI is read-only decision support. It never controls, silences or overrides fire systems (D-015). This is a design rule, not a tested result, and we do not claim the AI predicts fires [1].
  - In the unseen hall, the verifier flagged 17 of 17 fires and also 11 of 11 nuisance events. The naive PM-trigger baseline gets the same 17 and 11. Stage-2 AUROC is 0.67 (REAL, `eval/fire/sensor_eval.json`).
  - 6 of the 17 thresholds in `fire_rules.json` are team-proposed [4].
- **Clog:**
  - All riser results are SIMULATED.
  - On the held-out test, the 2.5 L/s test raised false alarms on 17.9% of clean nights with the z-score rule and 24.7% with gradient boosting. The thresholds aimed at about 5%. The 4.0 L/s z-score test raised 3.9% (`eval/clog/riser_metrics.json`).
  - Bellinge is one event in a municipal combined sewer, not a building drain. Its rule settings were chosen after the event was known [4].

  - The model is worse than the simple rules at naming the single wettest wall: 0.350 against 0.400. On real GFS forecasts it is not clearly better: MAE 3.64 against 3.85, with a 95% CI on the difference that crosses zero (`eval/rain/metrics.json`).
